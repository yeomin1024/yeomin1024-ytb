-도입
One thought you have when stocks rise can wreck your account in the next crash.
Mr. Lee grew his account to 100 million won with a single stock, and people called him a pro.
4 years later, that 100 million won had become 32 million won.
And that's while the S&P 500 rose more than 50%.
What broke Mr. Lee wasn't the crash itself, but the thoughts he had every time stocks rose.
Facing the five thoughts that came to Mr. Lee, choose what you would do.
Each time you choose, just count whether you thought the same way he did.
In the shoes of Mr. Lee, the so-called pro, you'll choose, starting with the first thought.
-선택 1
A year ago, you put all your savings into one stock.
It rose nearly 5x in a year, and your account hit 100 million won.
That's because COVID boosted remote work and online classes, and a video-call company's stock soared.
The S&P 500 rose 16% that same year.
People call you a stock pro and ask you for your next pick.
So, do you keep this stock, saying you made the money by picking well?
Or do you sell part of it to lock in your gains, saying you just got lucky riding the mood?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to call it luck and sell part of it to lock in your gains.
The date was December 31, 2020, and the stock was the video-call company Zoom.
Had you kept it like Mr. Lee, believing it was skill, Zoom would then have fallen as much as 84%.
Even 4 years later, you'd still be down 76%.
Of course, some really do profit through skill.
But skill or luck shows up not when stocks rise, but when they fall.
Of the companies in today's S&P 500, 14 rose more than 2x in 2020.
9 of them saw their stock price cut in half within 2 years.
Mistake luck for skill, and you'll bet even bigger on one stock.
In the end, the right choice is to see big gains as luck, and sell part of the winner to lock them in.
-선택 2
6 weeks later, the stock rose again, and your account grew another 28%.
You're wondering where to put the 28 million won you made in the meantime.
You're eyeing a fitness equipment maker whose stock rose more than 5x in a year.
Home workouts were all the rage, and its indoor bikes sold like hotcakes.
The internet is full of posts about buying stocks like this with gains and multiplying them again.
So, do you guard your gains like principal and not buy this fitness equipment maker?
Or do you buy this company with that money, saying it's fine to lose your gains?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to guard your gains like your principal and not buy this company.
The date was February 12, 2021, and the fitness equipment maker was Peloton.
Had you bought Peloton with your gains like Mr. Lee, 95% would have vanished by the end of 2022.
Even at the end of 2024, you'd still be down 94%.
Of course, gains feel like money you never had, so losing them hurts less.
But the money in your account, gains or principal, is all equally yours.
Money that has lost 94% must rise nearly 18x to get back to where it was.
In the end, the right choice is to treat gains like principal and not bet them on trendy stocks.
-선택 3
Seven months later, your stock is down 54% from its peak.
You're thinking of selling all of it and moving the money into a big company everyone knows.
That company's stock rose nearly 2x in a year and a half.
Subscribers to its new streaming service are growing fast, too.
People say a company like this will never go under, so it's safe to put it all in one place.
So, do you split the money across several companies, since even a big company can be cut in half?
Or do you put all the money into this company, since a big name everyone knows is safe?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to spread your money, since even a big company can be halved.
The date was September 30, 2021, and the company was Disney.
Had you put all your Zoom money into Disney like Mr. Lee, it would then have fallen as much as 53%.
Even at the end of 2024, you'd still be down 33%.
Of course, big companies don't go under as easily as small ones.
But a company surviving and its stock not falling are two different things.
The next year, in 2022, Meta fell as much as 74%, and Amazon as much as 51%.
Being a famous name doesn't make one company safe enough to put everything in.
In the end, the right choice is to know big companies can fall hard too, and not go all in on one.
-선택 4
Three months later, at year-end, the S&P 500 had set 70 new record highs that year.
The news says markets are flush with cash and predicts more gains next year.
You're thinking of borrowing 20 million won on a credit line to buy more of the index.
Your math: the index will rise more than the loan interest.
So, do you borrow to buy more of the index, saying it won't fall much anytime soon?
Or do you not borrow, saying that after rising this much, it could fall hard anytime?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to accept it could fall hard anytime, and not borrow.
The date was December 31, 2021, and three days later the index peaked and began to fall.
Had you borrowed to buy more like Mr. Lee, that money would have shrunk 24% by October of the next year.
Getting back to even would have taken nearly 2 years, with interest going out every month.
Without debt, all you'd have had to do was ride out the drop.
Of course, the index did rise again in the end, so borrowing to buy may seem fine.
But borrowed money comes with interest and a due date, which makes holding on in a drop far harder.
We looked at the 716 times the S&P 500 hit a record high since 1990.
One time in three, it fell more than 10% below that day's level within 2 years.
The longer record highs keep coming, the more you should plan ahead for a big drop.
In the end, the right choice is to know stocks can fall even in a rally, and not take on debt.
-선택 5
A month later, the stock you sold four months earlier is at just over 1/4 of its peak.
Online forums are full of posts saying that, compared with the peak, it's a bargain now.
You're thinking of pulling your money out of the index to buy this stock again.
So, do you not buy it back, saying the peak was a price made by a passing mood?
Or do you buy it back with money from the index, saying the peak was its true worth?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to see the peak as a passing mood, and not buy it back.
The date was January 31, 2022, and the stock was Zoom once again.
Had you bought it again like Mr. Lee because it looked cheap, it would then have fallen as much as 64%.
Even at the end of 2024, you'd still be down 47%.
Of course, a stock that has fallen far from its peak can't help looking cheap.
But that peak may have been a price set by the mood when everyone piled in.
To get from 1/4 of the peak back to the peak, it has to rise 3.7x.
Zoom is still at just over 1/6 of its peak today.
In the end, the right choice is not to judge cheap or expensive by the peak.
-결과
In this test, how many times did you think like Mr. Lee?
If never, you stay calm when stocks rise and prepare for crashes in advance: Pass.
If once or twice, you know how to prepare, but your thinking wavers when you're in profit: Caution.
If three times or more, like Mr. Lee, you're likely to end up losing even when you profit: Fail.
