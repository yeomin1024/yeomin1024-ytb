How do you borrow against your stocks without selling them? I recently moved a significant portion of my investment portfolio to Interactive Brokers so I could access cash through a margin loan instead of selling shares and immediately realizing capital gains.

This strategy is often called Buy, Borrow, Die. Rather than selling stocks, ETFs or other investments whenever you need money, you use the assets inside your brokerage account as collateral. You can then borrow money against your stock portfolio while your investments remain in the market.

I break down how to borrow against your stocks, how a portfolio loan works, why I chose Interactive Brokers, and the risks that come with using margin as a long-term borrowing strategy.

You’ll learn how to get a loan from your stocks, what happens when your brokerage cash balance becomes negative, how margin interest is calculated, and why a margin loan is different from a traditional securities-backed line of credit, also known as an SBLOC.

I also explain the Buy, Borrow, Die strategy, including how wealthy investors borrow against assets instead of selling them, why borrowed money generally is not treated as taxable income, and how the step-up in basis can affect inherited investments.

My personal goal is not to use margin to buy more speculative stocks. I’m using my long-term portfolio as collateral to access cash without constantly selling investments. That distinction matters because borrowing against a diversified stock portfolio is very different from aggressively using leverage to trade.

One of the biggest dangers is a margin call or forced liquidation. If the value of your investments falls too far relative to the amount borrowed, your broker may automatically sell positions. There may be no traditional warning period or opportunity to negotiate like you would have with a bank loan.

Interest rates can also change. A portfolio that earns more than the borrowing cost over a long period may make the strategy appear attractive, but investment returns are never guaranteed. The market could remain flat or decline while interest continues to accrue.

This is why I would never treat the maximum amount a broker allows you to borrow as the amount you should borrow. Maintaining a substantial safety cushion is one of the most important parts of managing a loan against stocks.
