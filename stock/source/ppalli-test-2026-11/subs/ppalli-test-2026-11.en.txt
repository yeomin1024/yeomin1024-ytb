You have 100 million won in savings.
Everyone's talking about a US tech stock that went up 3x in a year.
It's a company that uses AI to approve loans, and it's up more than 5x this year alone.
On the day a big investment firm agreed to buy a large batch of its loans, it jumped 35% in a single day.
Your coworkers and friends say they bought it, and they're bragging about their gains.
YouTube is flooded with videos saying it will go even higher.
So, do you buy this tech stock like everyone else?
Or do you skip buying this stock?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, not buying is the right choice.
The date was August 1, 2023, and the stock was Upstart.
If you bought like everyone else, as Mr. Kim did, it fell as much as 71%, and you'd still be down 67% today.
Of course, when everyone says it's good, it seems there must be a reason.
But by the time everyone is praising it, it has often already risen a lot.
This stock had already gone up 3x in a year, so buying it also meant buying the risk of an equally big fall.
So the right choice is not to buy just because others do.

-선택 2
On the same day, you have one more decision to make.
How much of the 100 million won do you put in?
Many people around you say holding cash is a loss, and that they put in all the money they had.
So, do you keep some cash aside for emergencies and invest only the rest?
Or do you invest all 100 million won?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, keeping cash aside for emergencies is the right choice.
That day was August 1, 2023, and in less than three months the S&P 500 fell 10%.
If you'd invested it all, like Mr. Kim, you'd have lost nearly 10%, even in the index.
If you'd suddenly needed a large sum then, you'd have had to sell your stocks at the lower price.
Of course, money kept in cash earns only interest while the market rises.
But worst cases, like losing your job or a housing problem, often come right when the market is bad.
With cash set aside, you can hold on without selling your stocks, even then.
So the right choice is to keep cash aside to prepare for the worst.

-선택 3
About a year and a half later, tariff fears pushed the S&P 500 down more than 10% from its peak.
The news is split between "it will fall further" and "this is the bottom."
Forums are full of screenshots of losses.
So, do you decide whether to buy or sell based on the news and the mood of the moment?
Or do you cut your position by a set rule, and buy back in parts each time it falls by a set percentage?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, cutting your position and buying back in parts with each drop is the right choice.
That day was March 13, 2025, when the S&P 500 was 10% below its peak.
What if, like Mr. Kim, you went by the mood and sold everything at the low on April 8?
If you only bought back after it regained its high 80 days later, you'd be up just 16% to date.
The numbers in a rule can differ from person to person.
For example, say you sell half that day and buy back 1/3 every time it falls another 5%.
Then on April 4, as it drops 5% and then 10% below your selling price, you buy back 2/3.
The remaining 1/3 you buy on the day the index regains its high.
Following this rule, you'd be up 46% from that day to now.
Of course, even with a rule, you can't know in advance where a fall will stop.
But if you set your rule in advance, you can make the same call even in scary moments.
So the right choice is to cut your position when it's risky, and buy back in parts as it falls.

-선택 4
About three months later, on an acquaintance's tip, you put half your account into an AI stock.
A little over a month later, the company reported far lower sales than expected and withdrew its earnings forecast.
Something went wrong with the company's basic health, its fundamentals.
The stock fell more than 25% in a single day.
So, since the company's fundamentals are broken, do you sell right away?
Or do you hold on until you get your money back?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, selling on the very day the company's problem appeared is the right choice.
That day was August 11, 2025, and the stock was C3.ai, which sells AI software to businesses.
If you held on like Mr. Kim, it fell another 53%, and it's still 25% below that day's price.
Had you sold right away, you'd have avoided that entire 53% drop.
Of course, selling after a drop of more than 25% in a day locks in the loss, and that feels painful.
But the price you paid has nothing to do with whether the stock will rise from here.
If the earnings behind the price collapse, holding on longer can deepen the loss.
One study looked at the trades of 10,000 individual investors in the US.
The winners they sold went on to beat the losers they kept by more than 3 percentage points over the next year.
If you admit the mistake and sell, you can start again with the money you have left.
So the right choice is to cut your losses right away when a company's fundamentals break.

-선택 5
Two months later, quantum computing stocks are hitting all-time highs day after day.
One of them is up more than 7x in a year.

3, 2, 1.
Here, capping one stock at 10% and spreading out the rest is the right choice.
That day was October 13, 2025, and the stock was IonQ, which builds quantum computers.
If you put in half like Mr. Kim, your account dropped 36% at one point, and you'd still be down 21% today.
With only 10% in it, your account would have dropped just 11% at its worst, and you'd be up 10% now.
Of course, the more you put in, the more you make when the stock rises.
But if that one bet is wrong, half your account goes down with it.
If you set a limit for each stock in advance, your account can survive even if one collapses.
So the right choice is to set a limit per stock, and spread your money across many stocks and sectors.
