People around you say, "Stocks are at a record high. Why worry?"

The more a growth stock is priced on future earnings, the harder it swings when rates rise.
So the right choice is to cut back on expensive growth stocks first when rates rise.

Two months later, oil prices soared to their highest level in over 13 years.
The price of a barrel of oil had jumped more than 60% in two months.
Oil prices signal both consumer prices and where the economy is heading.
Meanwhile, the S&P 500 had fallen 13%, and your growth stock 62%.
More and more people say there'll never be another chance to buy this cheap, and even borrow to buy.

The 3-month T-bill yield, which moves with loan rates, also rose from 0.4% to 4% in eight months.

But when oil pushes up inflation, rates tend to rise faster.
Borrowing then leaves you with rising interest and losses at once.
So the right choice is to expect rates to rise when oil soars, and to build up cash.

Eight months later, this time the signal came from the bond market.
The yield on 10-year Treasuries fell below the yield on three-month bills.
Normally, the longer you lend, the more you earn.
This is called a yield-curve inversion, and it's widely seen as a recession signal.
The S&P 500 is 18% below its record high.
The news is all about a coming recession, and more people around you say they've sold everything.
Do you decide the inversion makes a recession certain, sell everything and wait?
Or do you take it only as a warning of higher risk, and hold to your planned allocation?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
In this phase, taking it only as a warning and holding to your planned allocation is the right choice.
The date was November 10, 2022, the day the yield curve inverted.
If you had sold everything and waited like Mr. Park, you'd have gained just 10% in interest over 2 years.
Holding the index, you might have dropped another 4%, but you'd have been up 56% 2 years later.
Of course, a big bear market has often followed this signal.
Since 1990, three of the four inversions were followed by a bear market.
But the decline started anywhere from one month to 14 months later.
And after the 2022 inversion, no bear market came.
An inversion doesn't give you the date of a drop; it's just a warning that risk has grown.
So the right choice is to read an inversion as a warning, not a sell signal, and keep your allocation.
