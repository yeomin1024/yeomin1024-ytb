-도입
In a crash, what separates accounts isn't whether you feel fear, but whether you have a plan.
Mr. Jeong, too, kept cash to buy in a crash and even wrote down a buying plan.
But when the crash actually came, he followed fear instead of the plan.
As a result, 4 years later, his 100 million won was down to just over 35 million.
Meanwhile, the index recovered from that crash and rose more than 40% over the same 4 years.
Facing fear, Mr. Jeong made five decisions; choose what you would have done.
Just count how many of your decisions gave in to fear.
Now put yourself in Mr. Jeong's shoes on the day fear first struck.

-선택 1
You have 100 million won that you won't need for more than 5 years.
On the day the index hit an all-time high, you put 60 million won into the S&P 500.
You kept the other 40 million won in cash, to buy if a crash came.
You also wrote a buying plan: how much to buy at each level of decline.
Two months later, news of war pushed the index more than 12% below its high.
The index showing how much US stocks are expected to swing is called the fear index, or VIX.
That fear index shot up to 36, nearly double its usual level.
Your 60 million won has shrunk to about 52 million, and the news predicts only further falls.
Right now, will you sell everything before you lose more, and buy back once the fear fades?
Or will you hold, and take out the buying plan you wrote down?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is not to sell, but to take out the buying plan you wrote down.
That day was March 7, 2022, eleven days after Russia invaded Ukraine.
Suppose you, like Mr. Jeong, sold everything and bought back when the fear index fell below 20.
By then the index had risen 9%, so the same money would have bought 8% fewer shares.
So even by the end of 2025, you'd have gained only 58%.
Had you not sold, you'd have been up 72% at the same point.
Of course, selling that day would have dodged the next drop for a while.
But by the time the fear feels over, prices have usually already risen.
Since 1990, the fear index has topped 35 seventeen times, and that happened in sixteen of them.
Here's an example of a plan.
Each time the index is 10%, 15%, 20% and 25% below its high, buy with 1/4 of your cash.
So the right choice: the bigger the fear, don't sell; take out the buying plan you wrote down.

-선택 2
3 weeks later, the fear index dropped below 20, its usual level.
Meanwhile, the index had rebounded nearly 9%.
Forums fill with posts showing that people who bought on debt already made big gains.
Your brokerage app pops up a notice: you can borrow on margin to buy more.
Right now, will you buy without debt, only with the money you set aside for investing?
Or will you borrow on margin and buy more?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to buy without debt, only with the money you set aside.
Suppose that, like Mr. Jeong, you bought back on March 28 and borrowed the same amount on margin to buy more.
When the index then fell 22%, your leveraged money would have shrunk by nearly half.
Without debt, you'd have lost 21% at that point.
Your stocks' value relative to the loan would also have dropped to 151%.
Just above the 140% line where the broker force-sells your stocks.
Of course, as long as the index only rises, borrowing lets you earn more.
But debt doubles your losses when prices fall, and interest is charged every single day.
In the four bear markets since 1990, buying the index with debt on day one led to a forced sale twice.
So the right choice is to buy only with money you set aside, keeping the strength to hold on if prices fall further.

-선택 3
6 weeks later, the index fell back to 17% below its high.
At that point, a drugmaker made famous by its COVID vaccine had fallen 72% in less than 1 year.
People say it'll never be this cheap again, and that buying now will multiply your money.
Right now, will you skip this stock and keep your money in the index?
Or will you sell part of your index holdings to buy this stock?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to skip this stock and keep your money in the index.
That day was May 9, 2022, and the stock was Moderna.
If, like Mr. Jeong, you had sold half your index to buy Moderna, it would briefly have rebounded 55% late that year.
But by the end of 2025, it would be 78% below that day's price.
Had you stayed in the index, you'd have been down only 10% at worst, and up 81% by then.
Of course, some stocks, like Netflix, were deeply down that day and then rose more than fivefold.
But among the hardest-hit stocks, there was no way to know that day which would recover.
Take the 24 current S&P 500 companies that were down the most that day.
Within the next 2 years, 15 of them fell more than 30% further at some point.
So the right choice is to stick with an index that holds many stocks, not one stock that looks cheap.

-선택 4
Three months later, the index had rallied 17% from its earlier low.
On TV, expert after expert says the bear market is over.
You still have cash you haven't invested yet.
Right now, will you decide the bottom has passed and invest all your remaining cash at once?
Or will you wait until the next price written in your plan arrives?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to wait for the next price written in your plan.
That day was August 12, 2022, when the index was up nearly 17% from its June low.
If, like Mr. Jeong, you had put all your cash in that day, it would have been down 16% at the low two months later.
The next price in the plan, 25% below the high, came on September 30.
That day's price was 16% cheaper than on the day you went all in.
Just over 3 years later, the all-in money would have grown 68%, while the money that waited would have doubled.
Of course, if that rally had been the real bottom, prices might have kept rising while you waited.
But in a bear market, a rally often doesn't mean the bottom is in.
In bear markets since the dot-com bubble, six of ten rallies of more than 15% were followed by a new low.
So the right choice is not to go all in on a rally, but to wait for the next planned price.

-선택 5
A month and a half later, the index slid again, below its previous low.
It's 24% below the high, and news that inflation won't come down stirs up fear again.
Everything you've bought so far is at a loss.
Forums are flooded with posts saying buying by the plan is useless.
Right now, will you check that this money won't affect your daily life, and keep following the plan?
Or will you decide the plan was wrong, sell all your index holdings and stop?
Make your choice.
3, 2, 1.
Here, the right choice is to check your daily life won't be affected, and keep following the plan.
That day was September 26, 2022, when the index broke below its June low again.
If, like Mr. Jeong, you had sold everything and stopped, interest would have grown your money only 16% over 3-plus years.
Had you kept to the plan, it would have dipped 2% more, then risen 96%.
Of course, when the low breaks again, it feels like the plan itself was wrong.
But the plan was never about calling the bottom; it was about buying in parts, only what you can bear.
In those same bear markets, all three times the low broke again, holding was better 4 years later.
If it's money you'll need soon, then protect your daily life before the plan.
So the right choice: even if the low breaks again, if it's spare money, stick with your original plan.

-결과
In this test, how many of your decisions gave in to fear?
None? You keep buying by the plan with spare money even in a crash: you pass.
One or two? You have a plan, but fear or rallies sometimes shake you: caution.
Three or more? Like Mr. Jeong, you're likely to lose money by failing to buy out of fear: you fail.

-정리
As this test shows, patience pays: sticking to your plan and buying through a crash is the way to get rich with stocks. Keep that in mind.
