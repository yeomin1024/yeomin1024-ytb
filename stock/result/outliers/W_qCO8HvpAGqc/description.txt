If dividend stocks fall 20% and the dividend holds, every dollar you invest buys 25% more monthly income than it did before the drop. That is the math behind "keep buying". The problem is that the advice never says where the money comes from, because a crash usually arrives with shorter hours, smaller bonuses and bigger bills.

In this video we hold one illustrative portfolio fixed: $400,000 in dividend funds paying about 3.2% ($1,067 a month), plus $1,500 a month from your paycheck. Then we follow it through a crash:

- What $100 a month of dividend income costs before and after a 20% and a 35% drop, and how a dividend cut (like 2008-2009) eats most of the discount
- What two years of crash buying really adds to your monthly check, and what pausing costs
- Why your savings rate is exactly the pay cut you can absorb before your buying stops
- The buyer most investors forget: dividends that keep arriving and reinvesting with no paycheck, no sale and no decision
- How one forced sale during a crash permanently removes income, and how big your cash buffer should be
- The line where everything flips: when your dividends become your paycheck, you need a buffer sized to a realistic cut, not to your whole budget
- The two dangerous versions: chasing yields that jumped because prices collapsed, and borrowing or emptying your emergency fund to buy

All examples are illustrative calculations, before tax, not guaranteed and not personal financial advice. Yields, distributions and dividend growth change over time, and actual results depend on your accounts, taxes and holdings.
