The 1987 stock market crash is one of the most misunderstood events in modern financial history. On Monday, October 19, 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average collapsed 508.32 points, a 22.6 percent drop in a single trading session. It remains the largest one-day percentage decline in US stock market history, worse than any single day of the 1929 crash. Nearly 500 billion dollars in American market value vanished in six and a half hours, and global losses reached roughly 1.7 trillion dollars, close to 10 percent of the entire world economy. The world called it Black Monday.

For nearly four decades, textbooks have repeated the same explanation. Portfolio insurance did it. Program trading did it. The computers did it. But that story is only half the truth. In this deep dive, we uncover the hidden political and macroeconomic forces that actually triggered the collapse.

We trace the crash back to the Plaza Accord of September 22, 1985, when US Treasury Secretary James Baker gathered finance ministers from West Germany, Japan, France, and the UK to engineer a controlled devaluation of the dollar. We follow the fallout as the dollar crashed 47 percent against the mark and 39 percent against the yen in two years. We examine how the Louvre Accord of February 22, 1987 tried to freeze that collapse in place and why it was doomed from the moment the ink dried.

You will learn how the US trade deficit figure of 15.68 billion dollars released on October 14, 1987 lit the fuse, how Baker's Sunday ultimatum to West Germany on October 18 turned a stressed market into a tinderbox, and how the Bundesbank's October rate hike detonated the final charge. We walk through the global cascade from Hong Kong's catastrophic opening collapse, through London's free fall, into the New York Stock Exchange where 11 of the 30 Dow stocks could not even open on time.

We also examine the response that saved the global economy. New Fed Chairman Alan Greenspan, two months into the job after taking over from Paul Volcker on August 11, 1987, issued a statement on the morning of October 20 that reshaped central banking. The Fed injected 17 billion dollars of liquidity, cut the federal funds rate by half a point, and quietly ordered banks to keep lending. The Dow recovered 288 points, or 57 percent of its Black Monday losses, in two sessions.

This video unpacks three layers of what really caused Black Monday. The macroeconomic layer, where unsustainable American deficits met incompatible German monetary policy. The political layer, where Baker's currency pressure campaign turned stress into panic. And the mechanical layer, where portfolio insurance and index arbitrage amplified selling into the worst single-day crash in financial history.

We cover the global damage, from Hong Kong's 45.8 percent collapse and four-day shutdown, to Australia's 41.8 percent fall, to New Zealand's 60 percent decline. We examine the Brady Report, modern circuit breakers at 7, 13, and 20 percent, and the Greenspan Put that still shapes markets today.

Most importantly, we draw the lesson financial educators skip past. Markets do not crash because of math. They crash when the political arrangements underpinning global capital flows break down. From 1987 to the Asian crisis of 1997, from 2008 to the European debt crisis, every major crisis follows the same script. Technology gets blamed. Politics is the real driver.
